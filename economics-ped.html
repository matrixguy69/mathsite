Price elasticity of demand: how responsive are buyers?

Definition, calculation, the five values of PED, the SPLAT determinants, the total revenue rule and what it all means for firm pricing decisions and government tax policy. Notes plus every past-paper question on the sheet, answered.

CAIE 2281 IGCSE 0455

1. What PED measures

The law of demand says that when price rises, quantity demanded falls. Economists care about by how much. Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price.

  • The response is measured in percentage terms, so products of different sizes can be compared.
  • Responsiveness is different for different products: salt barely reacts to a price change, foreign holidays react strongly.
  • PED is almost always negative (price up, quantity demanded down), but we usually quote the absolute value.
Exam trapA question says "demand for petrol is elastic". This is shorthand: it is quantity demanded that responds to price. In answers, write "quantity demanded" whenever you are talking about a movement along the curve.

2. Calculating PED

PED = % change in quantity demanded% change in price = %ΔQD%ΔP
% change = new value − original valueoriginal value × 100

Always take the absolute value of the answer: ignore the minus sign and quote the size. A PED of −2 has a value of 2.

Worked example 1

The price of a bus ticket rises from £2 to £2.50 and quantity demanded falls from 1,000 to 900 tickets a day. Calculate PED.

  1. Price change: £0.50 ÷ £2 × 100 = 25% rise.
  2. Quantity change: −100 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = −10% fall.
  3. PED = −10% / 25% = −0.4
  4. Absolute value: 0.4, which is less than 1, so demand is price-inelastic.

PED = 0.4 (price-inelastic)

Worked example 2

A bakery cuts the price of cakes from £1.00 to £0.80 and sales rise from 200 to 300 a day. Calculate PED.

  1. Price change: −£0.20 ÷ £1.00 × 100 = −20%
  2. Quantity change: +100 ÷ 200 × 100 = +50%
  3. PED = 50% / −20% = −2.5
  4. Absolute value: 2.5, greater than 1, so demand is price-elastic.

PED = 2.5 (price-elastic)

Exam tipThe base for the percentage is always the original value. Show your percentage working even if the final number is wrong: method marks are real marks.

3. The five values of PED

Perfectly inelastic0Quantity demanded does not respond at all to priceHeart transplants (near enough); life-saving drugs with no substitute

Relatively elasticgreater than 1%ΔQD is more than proportional to %ΔPLuxuries, holidays, branded perfume, anything with close substitutes

Reading the slope safelySteep curve means inelastic, flat curve means elastic, but only when comparing curves drawn on the same axes with the same scales. In essays, argue from the determinants (see section 4), not from the picture alone.

4. The determinants of PED (SPLAT)

Why is one product responsive to price and another not? Five factors decide it. Learn them as SPLAT: Substitutes, Proportion of income, Luxury or necessity, Addiction, Time.

DeterminantMore elastic when...WhyExam example
S: SubstitutesGood availability of substitutesBuyers can switch away easily when price risesOne brand of biscuits: elastic. Biscuits as a whole: inelastic
P: Proportion of incomeThe price is a large share of incomeBig-ticket items force buyers to thinkA holiday: elastic. Salt: inelastic (a tiny share of income)
L: Luxury or necessityThe good is a luxuryYou can simply stop buying luxuriesDesigner perfume: elastic. Basic soap: inelastic
A: AddictionThe product is addictiveAddiction turns the product into a necessity for the buyerCigarettes: highly inelastic
T: Time periodMore time is allowedGiven time, consumers find substitutes and change habitsPetrol after a price rise: inelastic in months, more elastic over years
Worked example 3

Explain why demand for soap is more price-inelastic than demand for a luxury brand of perfume. (Q3(b)/22/M/J/22)

  1. Soap is a necessity with few close substitutes and a low price as a proportion of income, so buyers keep buying it when price rises: inelastic.
  2. Luxury perfume is a luxury, not essential, and has many substitutes (other brands, aftershaves, simply not wearing it).
  3. Therefore a given % price rise causes a much larger % fall in quantity demanded for perfume than for soap.

Soap: necessity, few substitutes, cheap share of income (inelastic). Perfume: luxury with many substitutes (elastic).

Worked example 4

Analyse why demand for a product may become more elastic over time. (Q2(c)/22/M/J/17)

  1. In the short run, consumers are locked into habits and current technology, so they keep buying: low PED.
  2. Given time they search for substitutes, invent alternatives, or change routines.
  3. The availability of substitutes rises, so the %ΔQD for a given %ΔP becomes larger.
  4. Example: after fuel price rises, people eventually buy smaller or electric cars, use public transport, or move house. Demand for petrol becomes more elastic the longer the high price lasts.

More time means more substitutes found and habits changed, so PED rises above 1 for many products.

Exam tip"Explain two reasons why demand is inelastic" means two different SPLAT letters, each with a one-line link to responsiveness. One determinant explained twice is one reason, not two.

5. PED and total revenue

Revenue is the money a firm receives from sales.

total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold = P × Q

The total revenue rule links PED to what happens to TR when price changes. Because price and quantity move in opposite directions, one effect on TR always dominates.

PEDPrice changeQuantity changeEffect on total revenue
Inelastic (0 to 1)Price increaseSmaller % fall in QDTR increases (price effect dominates)
Inelastic (0 to 1)Price decreaseSmaller % rise in QDTR decreases
Elastic (above 1)Price increaseLarger % fall in QDTR decreases (quantity effect dominates)
Elastic (above 1)Price decreaseLarger % rise in QDTR increases
Unit elastic (1)Any changeSame % changeTR unchanged
The rule in one lineIncrease price on inelastic products, decrease price on elastic products, to maximise revenue. If you remember nothing else from this chapter, remember that sentence.
Worked example 5

Analyse how price elasticity of demand for a product influences the revenue a firm receives. (Q4(c)/22/M/J/18)

  1. TR = P × Q. A price rise raises TR through price but cuts QD, so the net effect depends on PED.
  2. If demand is inelastic, the % price rise exceeds the % fall in quantity demanded, so TR rises. A firm selling an inelastic product should raise price.
  3. If demand is elastic, the % fall in quantity demanded exceeds the % price rise, so TR falls. A firm should cut price instead: quantity rises by more than proportion, and TR rises.
  4. At unit elasticity neither move changes TR, so revenue is already at its maximum.
  5. Therefore knowing PED tells the firm which direction to move price to maximise revenue.

Inelastic: raise price to raise TR. Elastic: cut price to raise TR. Unit elastic: TR is already at a maximum.

Worked example 6

Analyse how an increase in the price of milk may affect the revenue earned by milk producers and soft drinks producers.

  1. Milk is a necessity, so its demand is price-inelastic: a price rise causes a smaller % fall in quantity demanded, so milk producers' revenue rises.
  2. Milk and soft drinks are substitutes in consumption. A higher price of milk shifts the demand for soft drinks right.
  3. Soft drinks producers now sell more at the original price, so their revenue rises too (unless they cut price in response, which also raises quantity sold).

Milk producers gain directly through inelastic demand; soft drinks producers gain indirectly through the rightward shift in demand for their product.

6. What PED means for firms

  • Pricing decisions: raise price on inelastic products (cigarettes, fuel, staples), cut price on elastic products (holidays, restaurant meals in a competitive town).
  • Price discrimination: charging different prices to different groups for the same product, because groups have different PEDs. Students and over-60s get cheaper cinema tickets (elastic demand); everyone else pays full price (inelastic demand). The firm gains more total revenue than a single price would earn.
  • Advertising: heavy advertising tries to build brand loyalty, which reduces the availability of substitutes and makes demand more inelastic, giving the firm more pricing power.
  • A firm with more elastic demand (many rivals, undifferentiated product) has to be careful with price rises: customers walk next door.
Worked example 7

Explain what effect more firms producing tyres would have on the PED of an individual firm's tyres. (Q2(b)/22/M/J/19)

  1. More firms means more close substitutes for any one brand of tyre.
  2. Buyers can switch brand easily if one firm raises its price.
  3. The PED of an individual firm's tyres becomes more elastic (the value rises above its previous level).
  4. Therefore each firm has less power to raise prices without losing sales.

More substitutes from rival firms make each firm's demand more price-elastic.

Worked example 8

Explain one way price elasticity of demand may influence firms' decision making. (Q1(c)/23/O/N/23)

  1. The firm estimates PED before changing price.
  2. If PED is elastic, it cuts price to win a more-than-proportional rise in sales and raise total revenue.
  3. If PED is inelastic, it raises price instead, raising revenue with only a small loss of sales.

PED tells the firm which way to move price: down when elastic, up when inelastic.

Exam trapRaising price on an elastic product raises revenue only if costs do not also jump. But for O Level marks, stick to the revenue rule: the examiner wants inelastic-up, elastic-down, nothing more complicated.

7. What PED means for governments

  • Taxation: taxing products with inelastic demand (cigarettes, fuel, alcohol) raises large, reliable tax revenue, because quantity demanded barely falls. The tax is passed on to consumers in higher prices, so firms are not destroyed and consumption only dips a little.
  • Subsidies: subsidising products with elastic demand (public transport, basic foods) produces a greater than proportional rise in quantity demanded, so the policy actually changes behaviour and consumption rises significantly at a lower price.
  • Merit and demerit goods: demerit goods (cigarettes) have inelastic demand, so tax works slowly on quantity but funds the health service; governments may need education campaigns as well.
Worked example 9

Explain the importance of price elasticity of demand for a government. (Q2(b)/22/O/N/16)

  1. Before setting an indirect tax or subsidy, the government needs the PED of the product.
  2. Taxing inelastic products maximises tax revenue with a small fall in quantity demanded: it funds public spending without heavily discouraging the taxed activity.
  3. Taxing elastic products barely raises revenue and kills sales, hurting both firms and consumers of the product.
  4. Subsidising elastic products achieves the biggest rise in consumption for the money spent.

PED guides tax and subsidy choices: inelastic goods for revenue, elastic goods for changing quantities consumed.

Worked example 10

Analyse how an increase in the price elasticity of demand and the price elasticity of supply of its products could benefit a firm. (Q5(c)/22/O/N/18)

  1. More elastic demand lets the firm gain customers quickly from rivals by cutting its price: it can grow market share.
  2. More elastic supply means the firm can expand output quickly (spare capacity, flexible inputs) to serve those new customers, without costs and prices exploding.
  3. Together, elastic demand and elastic supply let the firm respond fast to opportunities and to falling input costs, selling more volume at competitive prices.

Elastic demand helps the firm win sales through price competition; elastic supply lets it deliver the extra output at manageable cost.

8. Practice set and answers

Do every question on paper first. Open an answer only after you have committed to one.

Set A · Determinants and values of PED

Analyse why demand for a product may become more elastic over time. Q2(c)/22/M/J/17
Time lets consumers discover and adopt substitutes and change habits, so %ΔQD for a given %ΔP grows. Example: fuel price rises eventually lead to electric cars and public transport use, raising PED for petrol.
Explain what effect more firms producing tyres would have on the PED of individual firms' tyres. Q2(b)/22/M/J/19
More firms means more close substitutes. If one firm raises price, buyers switch to rivals, so its demand becomes more elastic and its pricing power falls.
Explain two reasons why demand for a product may be price-inelastic. Q3(b)/23/O/N/19
(1) Few or no close substitutes (e.g. insulin), so buyers cannot switch. (2) The product is a necessity or addictive (e.g. cigarettes), so buyers keep purchasing when price rises. Any two of: substitutes, necessity, addiction, small share of income, short time period.
Discuss whether or not demand for cars will become more price-elastic in the future. Q4(d)/22/O/N/20
For: more substitutes develop (public transport, ride-sharing, electric alternatives, city living), and cars take a large share of income, so buyers react more. Against: for many, a car remains a necessity with weak public transport alternatives, and brand loyalty keeps demand loyal. A balanced discussion states both sides and judges which is stronger in a given context.
Explain why demand for soap is more price-inelastic than demand for a luxury brand of perfume. Q3(b)/22/M/J/22
Soap is a cheap necessity with few substitutes: buyers keep buying it when price rises. Luxury perfume is a luxury with many substitutes (other brands, not buying any): a price rise cuts quantity demanded by more than proportion.

Set B · PED, revenue and the firm

Analyse how price elasticity of demand for a product influences the revenue a firm receives. Q4(c)/22/M/J/18
TR = P × Q. If demand is inelastic, a price rise causes a smaller % fall in QD, so TR rises. If elastic, a price rise causes a larger % fall in QD, so TR falls (and cutting price does the opposite). At unit elasticity TR is maximised.
Analyse how an increase in the price elasticity of demand and the price elasticity of supply of its products could benefit a firm. Q5(c)/22/O/N/18
Elastic demand: price cuts win a large rise in sales, letting the firm take market share. Elastic supply: the firm can expand output quickly to meet the new demand without steep cost increases. Both together mean faster, cheaper growth.
Analyse how a change in the PED for its products may benefit a firm. Q6(c)/21/M/J/19
If PED falls (more inelastic, e.g. after successful advertising builds loyalty), the firm can raise prices without losing many sales, increasing revenue and profit. If PED rises, the firm can cut prices to attract many new customers, raising sales volume.
Analyse how an increase in the price of milk may affect the revenue earned by milk producers and soft drinks producers. (own question, from source sheet)
Milk demand is inelastic, so a price rise raises milk producers' total revenue. Milk and soft drinks are substitutes: demand for soft drinks shifts right, so soft drinks producers sell more at the original price and their revenue rises too.
Explain one way price elasticity of demand may influence firms' decision making. Q1(c)/23/O/N/23
It decides the direction of price changes: firms raise prices on inelastic products (few sales lost) and cut prices on elastic products (sales rise by more than proportion, raising revenue).

Set C · PED and government, plus calculation drills

Explain the importance of price elasticity of demand for a government. Q2(b)/22/O/N/16
PED shows which goods to tax and subsidise. Taxing inelastic goods (cigarettes, fuel) raises large, stable tax revenue with little fall in consumption. Subsidising elastic goods gives the biggest rise in quantity consumed per dollar spent.
Price rises from £4 to £5 and quantity demanded falls from 500 to 420. Calculate PED and interpret it. (own drill)
%ΔP = 1/4 × 100 = 25%. %ΔQD = −80/500 × 100 = −16%. PED = −16/25 = −0.64, value 0.64: demand is price-inelastic, so a price rise would increase total revenue.
Price falls from £10 to £8 and quantity demanded rises from 1,000 to 1,500. Calculate PED and say what a further price cut would do to revenue. (own drill)
%ΔP = −2/10 × 100 = −20%. %ΔQD = 500/1,000 × 100 = +50%. PED = 50/−20 = −2.5, value 2.5: elastic. A further price cut would increase total revenue.
Explain why governments often tax cigarettes heavily even though demand is inelastic. (own drill)
Inelastic demand means the tax can be passed on in higher prices with only a small fall in quantity demanded, so tax revenue is large and reliable. The higher price still deters some young smokers, helping the health objective too.

9. Quick quiz

Click an option to check your answer.

MCQ 1 Price rises by 10% and quantity demanded falls by 2%. PED is:

Answer: B. PED = 2% ÷ 10% = 0.2. A value below 1 means quantity demanded is unresponsive: inelastic.

MCQ 2 Demand for a product is price-inelastic. The firm wants to raise total revenue. It should:

Answer: C. With inelastic demand the % price rise exceeds the % fall in quantity demanded, so total revenue increases.

MCQ 3 Which change would make the demand for a product more price-elastic?

Answer: A. More substitutes let buyers switch when price rises, raising PED. The other options all make demand more inelastic.

MCQ 4 A government wants to raise tax revenue while keeping consumption of the taxed good nearly unchanged. It should tax a good with:

Answer: D. Inelastic demand means quantity demanded barely falls when the tax pushes price up, so revenue is large and stable.

MCQ 5 A price cut leaves total revenue exactly unchanged. Demand is:

Answer: B. At PED = 1 the % change in quantity demanded exactly offsets the % change in price, so total revenue stays the same.

10. Exam checklist

  • Defined PED as the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.
  • Used %ΔQD ÷ %ΔP with the original value as the base, and showed the percentage working.
  • Quoted PED as an absolute value and classified it (in/unit/elastic) in the same breath.
  • Named the determinant (SPLAT) with a one-line link to responsiveness whenever asked "why".
  • Used the total revenue rule correctly: raise price when inelastic, cut price when elastic.
  • Linked price discrimination to groups with different PEDs.
  • Linked government tax revenue to inelastic demand and subsidies to elastic demand.
  • Answered "over time" questions with the substitutes-and-habits argument.
  • For "discuss" questions, argued both sides before judging.
