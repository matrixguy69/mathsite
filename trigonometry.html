1. Triangles you must know

Every trigonometry question is built on triangles. Before you touch sin, cos or tan, you must be able to name the triangle you have and find any missing angle in it. There are three facts that never change.

angles in a triangle add up to 180°
a straight line adds up to 180° · angles at a point add up to 360°

1.1 Equilateral triangles

  • All three sides equal, all three angles equal.
  • Each angle is 180° ÷ 3 = 60°.
  • If each side is a, the vertical height is a√3 / 2 (drop a perpendicular from the top vertex: it splits the triangle into two 30-60-90 right triangles).
Worked example 1

An equilateral triangle has side 14 cm. Find all three angles and its vertical height.

  1. All three angles are equal and add to 180°: each is 60°.
  2. Height: split it down the middle to get a right triangle with hypotenuse 7 cm.
  3. h² + 7² = 14², so h² = 196 − 49 = 147 and h = √147 = 7√3
  4. 7 × 1.732 ≈ 12.1

All angles 60°, height = 7√3 ≈ 12.1 cm

1.2 Isosceles triangles

  • Two equal sides, and the two base angles (the angles between a equal side and the base) are equal.
  • Given one base angle, double it and subtract from 180° to get the top (vertex) angle.
  • Given the vertex angle, subtract it from 180° and halve to get each base angle.
Worked example 2

An isosceles triangle has two equal sides of length 9 cm and 9 cm, and the angle between them is 40°. Find the two base angles.

  1. The angle between the equal sides is the vertex angle: 40°.
  2. Remaining angle for the two equal base angles: 180° − 40° = 140°
  3. They are equal, so each is 140° ÷ 2 = 70°

Both base angles are 70°

Exam trapNot every triangle with two equal-looking angles in a diagram is isosceles. Only assume equal angles when they are marked, stated in the question, or forced by the geometry (for example, a radius to a chord creating equal base angles).

1.3 Scalene and right-angled triangles

  • A scalene triangle has no equal sides and no equal angles. You always need one more fact (a side and an angle) before trig kicks in.
  • A right-angled triangle has one angle of 90°, so the two acute angles add to 90°. That single fact solves many one-mark questions on its own.
Worked example 3

A right-angled triangle has one acute angle of 37°. Find the other acute angle.

  1. The three angles sum to 180° and one of them is 90°.
  2. The two acute angles sum to 90°.
  3. 90° − 37° = 53°

The other acute angle is 53°

Exam tipWhenever a diagram shows a triangle inside a rectangle, a ladder against a wall, or a pole against the ground, look for the hidden right angle first. Right angles unlock both Pythagoras and SOH CAH TOA.

2. Pythagoras theorem

In any right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse (the longest side, always opposite the 90° angle) equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

a² + b² = c²    (c = hypotenuse)
  • Hypotenuse: opposite the right angle, always the longest side. Label it c first, before anything else.
  • Finding the hypotenuse: add the two squares, then square root.
  • Finding a shorter side: subtract. If c is known, use a² = c² − b².

2.1 Finding the hypotenuse

Worked example 4

A right-angled triangle has shorter sides 9 cm and 12 cm. Find the hypotenuse.

  1. Label the hypotenuse c (it is opposite the right angle).
  2. c² = 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225
  3. c = √225 = 15

Hypotenuse = 15 cm

2.2 Finding a shorter side

Worked example 5

A ladder is 13 m long and its foot stands 5 m from the base of a wall. How far up the wall does it reach?

  1. The ladder is the hypotenuse: c = 13. Let h be the height up the wall.
  2. h² + 5² = 13²
  3. h² = 169 − 25 = 144
  4. h = √144 = 12

The ladder reaches 12 m up the wall

2.3 When NOT to use Pythagoras

Exam trapPythagoras only works in triangles that contain a right angle. If the triangle is scalene or obtuse with no 90° marked, you need the sine rule or the cosine rule instead. Substituting into a² + b² = c² in a non-right triangle is a guaranteed lost mark.

2.4 Classic exam setups

3. Trigonometric ratios in right-angled triangles

The three trig ratios connect an angle in a right triangle to ratios of its sides. Every ratio depends on which angle you are standing at, so labelling comes first.

3.1 Labelling the sides (relative to your angle θ)

SideHow to identify it
HypotenuseOpposite the right angle. Always the longest side.
OppositeOpposite your angle θ (the side that does not touch θ).
AdjacentNext to θ, and not the hypotenuse (the other side that touches θ).
sin θ = oppositehypotenuse  ·  cos θ = adjacenthypotenuse  ·  tan θ = oppositeadjacent
  • SOH: Sine = Opposite over Hypotenuse
  • CAH: Cosine = Adjacent over Hypotenuse
  • TOA: Tangent = Opposite over Adjacent

3.2 Exact values you must memorise

Anglesincostan
30°1/2√3/2 ≈ 0.871/√3 ≈ 0.58
45°√2/2 ≈ 0.71√2/2 ≈ 0.711
60°√3/2 ≈ 0.871/2√3 ≈ 1.73
How to remember itsin 30° = 1/2 and sin 60° = √3/2, and they swap for cos (cos 30° = √3/2, cos 60° = 1/2). tan 45° = 1 because opposite and adjacent are equal in a 45-45-90 triangle. If a question allows exact answers, give the surd form, not the decimal.

3.3 Finding a side (multiply or divide)

If the side you want is on top of the ratio, multiply. If it is underneath, divide. The pattern is always: unknown = (known side) ÷ (or ×) (the ratio).

Worked example 6

A right triangle has an angle of 30°. The side opposite the 30° angle is 6 cm. Find the adjacent side.

  1. We have opposite and want adjacent: use TOA, tan θ = opposite / adjacent.
  2. tan 30° = 6 / x
  3. Rearrange: x = 6 / tan 30° = 6 / 0.5774
  4. x ≈ 10.39

Adjacent side ≈ 10.4 cm (exact: 6√3)

3.4 Finding an angle (inverse sin, cos, tan)

To get an angle back from a ratio, use the inverse button: sin−1, cos−1 or tan−1 (on many calculators it is shift + sin, shift + cos, shift + tan).

Worked example 7

In a right triangle the side opposite angle A is 5 cm and the hypotenuse is 10 cm. Find angle A.

  1. Opposite and hypotenuse: use SOH.
  2. sin A = 5 / 10 = 0.5
  3. A = sin−1(0.5)
  4. Exact value: A = 30°

Angle A = 30°

Exam tipEvery O Level trigonometry question assumes your calculator is in DEGREES mode, not radians. Check the little D indicator on screen before every calculation. A radian-mode answer like 0.52 instead of 30° costs the whole question.
Exam trapsin−1(0.5) = 30° is an angle. (sin 0.5)−1 or 1 / sin 0.5 is something else entirely. Press the inverse button first, then the ratio.

4. Trig ratio practice: multi-step problems

Real exam questions rarely stop after one step. The common patterns are: Pythagoras then a ratio, two right triangles sharing a side, and word problems where you must draw and label the triangle yourself.

Worked example 8

A ladder 6.5 m long leans against a vertical wall. Its foot is 2.5 m from the base of the wall. Find the angle the ladder makes with the ground, to 1 dp.

  1. Draw the right triangle: wall, ground, ladder as hypotenuse. Height up the wall is h.
  2. Pythagoras first: h² = 6.5² − 2.5² = 42.25 − 6.25 = 36, so h = 6 m
  3. At the ground angle θ: opposite = 6, hypotenuse = 6.5 (or adjacent = 2.5).
  4. tan θ = 6 / 2.5 = 2.4
  5. θ = tan−1(2.4) ≈ 67.4°

The ladder leans at about 67.4° to the ground

Worked example 9

A kite string is 25 m long and makes an angle of 55° with the horizontal ground. Find the height of the kite and its horizontal distance from the person holding it.

  1. Right triangle: string = hypotenuse (25 m), angle with the ground = 55°.
  2. Height is the opposite side: sin 55° = h / 25
  3. h = 25 × sin 55° = 25 × 0.8192 ≈ 20.5
  4. Horizontal distance is the adjacent side: d = 25 × cos 55° = 25 × 0.5736 ≈ 14.3

Height ≈ 20.5 m, horizontal distance ≈ 14.3 m

Worked example 10

A rectangle measures 7 cm by 9 cm. Find the length of its diagonal and the angle the diagonal makes with the longer side.

  1. Corners of a rectangle are 90°, so the diagonal is a hypotenuse.
  2. d² = 7² + 9² = 49 + 81 = 130, so d = √130 ≈ 11.4
  3. The angle with the longer (9 cm) side has opposite = 7.
  4. tan θ = 7 / 9 ≈ 0.7778
  5. θ = tan−1(0.7778) ≈ 37.9°

Diagonal ≈ 11.4 cm, angle with the long side ≈ 37.9°

Practice set

A right triangle has shorter sides 5 cm and 11 cm. Find the hypotenuse.
c² = 25 + 121 = 146, so c = √146 ≈ 12.1 cm.
A right triangle has hypotenuse 15 cm and one side 9 cm. Find the angle between them, to 1 dp.
Adjacent 9, hypotenuse 15: cos θ = 9/15 = 0.6, so θ = cos−1(0.6) ≈ 53.1°.
A ramp 4 m long reaches a step 1.5 m high. Find its angle of elevation, to 1 dp.
Opposite 1.5, hypotenuse 4: sin θ = 1.5/4 = 0.375, so θ = sin−1(0.375) ≈ 22.0°.
A ship sails 12 km due east, then 5 km due north. Find its direct distance from the start, and the bearing it would need to sail home.
Distance = √(12² + 5²) = √169 = 13 km. From the ship, home is 12 km west and 5 km south, so the bearing is 180° + tan−1(12/5) ≈ 180° + 67.4° = 247° (to the nearest degree).

5. The sine rule

Right-triangle trig only works when a 90° angle exists. The sine rule works in any triangle, and it links each side to the angle opposite it.

a / sin A = b / sin B = c / sin C

Here a is the side opposite angle A, b opposite B, and c opposite C. To find an angle, flip the fractions:

sin A / a = sin B / b = sin C / c

5.1 When to use it

The sine rule testUse the sine rule when you have (or want) a matching pair: a side and the angle directly opposite it. With one complete pair plus one extra side or angle, the sine rule is the right tool. If no pair exists, use the cosine rule instead.

5.2 Finding a side

Worked example 11

Triangle ABC has A = 50°, a = 10 cm and B = 60°. Find side b to 1 dp.

  1. Pair: angle A = 50° with side a = 10 opposite it. Good for sine rule.
  2. a / sin A = b / sin B
  3. 10 / sin 50° = b / sin 60°
  4. b = (10 × sin 60°) / sin 50° = (10 × 0.8660) / 0.7660
  5. b = 8.660 / 0.7660 ≈ 11.31

b ≈ 11.3 cm

5.3 Finding an angle

Worked example 12

Triangle ABC has A = 52°, a = 8 m and b = 10 m. Find angle B.

  1. Use the angle form: sin B / b = sin A / a
  2. sin B = (b × sin A) / a = (10 × sin 52°) / 8
  3. sin B = (10 × 0.7880) / 8 = 7.880 / 8 = 0.9850
  4. B = sin−1(0.9850) ≈ 80.0°
  5. Or the obtuse alternative: 180° − 80.0° = 100.0°

B ≈ 80.0° or 100.0° (check the diagram for which fits)

The obtuse alternativeSince sin(180° − θ) = sin θ, a sine-rule angle can have two valid answers: θ and 180° − θ. Both are possible unless the diagram or the other angles rule one out (a triangle can only have one angle of 90° or more). Write both when the question is ambiguous, and always give the answer the diagram supports.

6. The cosine rule

The cosine rule is Pythagoras for triangles that are not right-angled. It has two forms: one to find a side, one to find an angle.

a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A
cos A = (b² + c² − a²) / (2bc)

Angle A must sit between sides b and c, and side a is the one opposite that angle. When A = 90°, cos A = 0 and the formula collapses back to Pythagoras.

6.1 Which rule? The decision rule

What you are givenMatched pair (side + opposite angle)?Use
Two angles + a side, or two sides + one of their opposite anglesYesSine rule
Two sides + the angle between them (SAS)NoCosine rule (side form)
All three sides (SSS)NoCosine rule (angle form)

6.2 Finding a side (SAS)

Worked example 13

Triangle ABC has b = 5 cm, c = 7 cm and the angle between them A = 60°. Find side a.

  1. Two sides and the included angle: cosine rule, side form.
  2. a² = 5² + 7² − 2 × 5 × 7 × cos 60°
  3. a² = 25 + 49 − 70 × 0.5
  4. a² = 74 − 35 = 39
  5. a = √39 ≈ 6.24

a ≈ 6.2 cm

6.3 Finding an angle (SSS)

Worked example 14

A triangle has sides 7 cm, 8 cm and 9 cm. Find the largest angle, to 1 dp.

  1. The largest angle sits opposite the longest side (9 cm).
  2. cos A = (7² + 8² − 9²) / (2 × 7 × 8)
  3. cos A = (49 + 64 − 81) / 112 = 32 / 112 ≈ 0.2857
  4. A = cos−1(0.2857) ≈ 73.4°

Largest angle ≈ 73.4°

Exam trapIn a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A, always subtract 2bc cos A as one chunk. Typing b² + c² − 2bc then × cos A on a calculator multiplies only the last term. Work out 2bc first, then the product, then subtract.
Exam tipThe cosine rule angle form only gives one answer between 0° and 180° from cos−1, and that answer is the correct one. The ambiguous two-angle situation belongs to the sine rule only.

7. Area of a triangle

You already know the basic formula. Trigonometry gives you a second formula that works for any triangle when you know two sides and the angle between them.

area = ½ × base × height   (when the height is known)
area = ½ ab sin C

In ½ ab sin C, sides a and b are the two sides you know, and C is the angle between them (the included angle). If the angle is 90°, sin 90° = 1 and the formula becomes ½ × base × height again.

Worked example 15

A triangle has sides 9 cm and 12 cm with an included angle of 38°. Find its area.

  1. Two sides and the angle between them: use ½ ab sin C.
  2. area = ½ × 9 × 12 × sin 38°
  3. = 54 × 0.6157
  4. ≈ 33.25

Area ≈ 33.2 cm² (1 dp)

Exam tipArea answers need square units: cm², m², km². And check the angle is the one between the two given sides before you substitute.

8. Angles of elevation and depression

These questions put a right triangle into a real-world scene. Both angles are measured from the horizontal, never from the vertical.

TermDefinition
Angle of elevationLooking up from the horizontal to an object above you (e.g. the top of a tower).
Angle of depressionLooking down from the horizontal to an object below you (e.g. a boat from a cliff top).
The key theoremThe angle of depression from A down to B equals the angle of elevation from B up to A. The reason is alternate angles: the two horizontal lines of sight are parallel, and the line of sight between A and B is the transversal cutting them. So you can always convert a depression problem into the (usually easier) elevation picture.
Worked example 16

From a point on the ground 35 m from the base of a tower, the angle of elevation to the top is 60°. Find the height of the tower.

  1. Right triangle: ground distance 35 m is the adjacent side, height h is the opposite side, angle 60°.
  2. tan 60° = h / 35
  3. h = 35 × tan 60° = 35 × √3 = 35 × 1.732
  4. h ≈ 60.6

Tower height ≈ 60.6 m

Worked example 17

A cliff is 45 m high. From the top, the angle of depression to a car on the road below is 28°. How far is the car from the base of the cliff?

  1. Angle of depression 28° from the horizontal at the top equals the angle of elevation of the cliff top from the car (alternate angles): 28°.
  2. Now the triangle at the car: opposite = 45 m, adjacent = d (distance from the base).
  3. tan 28° = 45 / d
  4. d = 45 / tan 28° = 45 / 0.5317 ≈ 84.6

The car is about 84.6 m from the base of the cliff

Exam trapDo not put the angle of elevation or depression inside the triangle at the wrong corner. It always sits at the observer's eye, measured from the horizontal line through the eye. Draw a small horizontal dashed line at the eye before you place the angle.

9. Bearings

Bearings describe direction. There are two systems, and the three-figure true bearing is the one the exam uses in calculations.

9.1 Three-figure (true) bearings

  • Measured clockwise from north (from the direction marked N on the diagram).
  • Always written with three digits, including leading zeros: 075°, 008°, 240°.
  • North itself is 000°, east is 090°, south is 180°, west is 270°.
  • The bearing of B from A is measured at A: draw a north line at A, then measure clockwise to the line AB.

9.2 Compass bearings

Compass bearingMeaningTrue bearing
N 40° EFrom north, 40° towards east040°
S 20° EFrom south, 20° towards east180° − 20° = 160°
S 70° WFrom south, 70° towards west180° + 70° = 250°
N 15° WFrom north, 15° towards west360° − 15° = 345°

9.3 How to work a bearings problem

  1. Draw a north line at every point mentioned. All north lines are parallel, so alternate and co-interior angles give you extra angles for free.
  2. Write in every angle you can deduce before you pick a rule.
  3. Look for a triangle you can solve with the sine rule (a matched pair) or the cosine rule (SAS or SSS). Bearings problems often build an isosceles triangle somewhere.
  4. Give final bearings to the nearest degree, in three figures.
Worked example 18

A ship sails from port P on a bearing of 070° for 12 km to point Q, then changes course and sails on a bearing of 150° for 8 km to point R. Find the distance PR and the bearing of R from P.

  1. Draw north lines at P, Q and R. Mark PQ at 070°, QR at 150°, with PQ = 12 and QR = 8.
  2. At Q, the back-bearing of P is 070° + 180° = 250°. The angle between QP (250°) and QR (150°) is 250° − 150° = 100°, so ∠PQR = 100°.
  3. Two sides and the included angle: cosine rule for PR. PR² = 12² + 8² − 2 × 12 × 8 × cos 100°
  4. PR² = 144 + 64 − 192 × (−0.1736) = 208 + 33.3 = 241.3, so PR ≈ 15.5 km
  5. For the bearing, find angle QPR with the sine rule: sin QPR / 8 = sin 100° / 15.535
  6. sin QPR = (8 × 0.9848) / 15.535 ≈ 0.507, so QPR ≈ 30.5°
  7. Bearing of R from P = 070° + 30.5° ≈ 100°

PR ≈ 15.5 km, bearing of R from P = 100°

Exam tipA back-bearing is always +180° (or −180° if the answer would exceed 360°). The bearing of A from B and the bearing of B from A differ by exactly 180°. Write the north lines first and this falls out automatically.

10. Mixed practice set and answers

Do every question on paper first. Open an answer only after you have committed to one.

An isosceles triangle has a vertex angle of 100°. Find each base angle.
Remaining angle = 180° − 100° = 80°, split equally between the two base angles. Each base angle is 40°.
A right triangle has hypotenuse 17 cm and one side 8 cm. Find the other side.
x² = 17² − 8² = 289 − 64 = 225, so x = √225 = 15 cm.
In a right triangle, the side opposite angle θ is 7.5 cm and the adjacent side is 10 cm. Find θ to 1 dp.
tan θ = 7.5 / 10 = 0.75, so θ = tan−1(0.75) ≈ 36.9°.
Triangle ABC has A = 45°, a = 6 cm and B = 75°. Find side b to 1 dp using the sine rule.
b = (6 × sin 75°) / sin 45° = (6 × 0.9659) / 0.7071 ≈ 5.80 / 0.7071 ≈ 8.2 cm.
A triangle has sides 5 cm, 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the angle opposite the 8 cm side, to 1 dp.
cos A = (5² + 6² − 8²) / (2 × 5 × 6) = (25 + 36 − 64) / 60 = −3 / 60 = −0.05. So A = cos−1(−0.05) ≈ 92.9°. Note the answer is obtuse, which makes sense: 8 is longer than 5 and 6, so its opposite angle is the largest.
Two sides of a triangle are 10 cm and 14 cm with an included angle of 52°. Find the area to 1 dp.
area = ½ × 10 × 14 × sin 52° = 70 × 0.7880 ≈ 55.2 cm².
Write these as three-figure bearings: (a) N 65° E, (b) S 20° E, (c) due west.
(a) 065° (from north, 65° towards east). (b) 180° − 20° = 160°, so 160°. (c) Due west is 270°.
From 30 m away from the base of a tower, the angle of elevation to the top is 45°. Find the height of the tower.
Opposite over adjacent with tan: tan 45° = h / 30 and tan 45° = 1, so h = 30 m. (Whenever the elevation is 45°, height equals distance: the triangle is isosceles.)
A rectangle is 12 m by 5 m. Find the length of its diagonal and the angle between the diagonal and the 12 m side, to 1 dp.
d = √(12² + 5²) = √169 = 13 m. Then tan θ = 5 / 12 ≈ 0.4167, so θ ≈ 22.6°.
In a triangle, sin of one of its angles is 0.4. List every possible size of that angle, to 1 dp.
θ = sin−1(0.4) ≈ 23.6°, or the obtuse alternative 180° − 23.6° = 156.4°. Both are possible angles of a triangle (the obtuse one only if the other two angles can fit in the remaining 23.6°).

11. Exam checklist

  • Calculator is in DEGREES mode before every trig calculation.
  • Labelled the hypotenuse, opposite and adjacent relative to the stated angle before choosing SOH, CAH or TOA.
  • Checked for a right angle before using Pythagoras or a ratio; used the sine or cosine rule when there is none.
  • Matched a side with the angle directly opposite it before reaching for the sine rule.
  • Checked the sine rule angle for the obtuse alternative 180° − θ when the diagram allows it.
  • Used the cosine rule when given SAS (two sides + included angle) or SSS (all three sides), and subtracted 2bc cos A as one term.
  • For area, used ½ ab sin C only with the angle between the two known sides, and gave square units.
  • Measured angles of elevation and depression from the horizontal, at the observer's eye.
  • Drew a north line at every point in a bearings problem and wrote bearings as three digits with a leading zero (075°, 240°).
  • Rounded final answers sensibly (usually 1 dp) and stated units on every answer.
