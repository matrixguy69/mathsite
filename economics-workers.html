Workers: wages, the labour market and specialisation

The full workers topic: wage and non-wage factors, derived demand, supply of labour, labour market diagrams, elasticity of labour, bargaining power, minimum wage, wage differentials and the division of labour.

CAIE 2281 IGCSE 0455

1. Wage factors: the financial payments

The occupational choice of a worker is decided by weighing wage factors against non-wage factors. Wage factors are the financial payments a worker receives for their labour. Learn every row of this table: the exam asks you to identify which type of pay a scenario describes, or to calculate with it.

Wage factorHow it works (with the example you should be able to calculate)
WagesAn agreed amount per hour, calculated from hours worked. A student working 6 hours a day, 5 days a week at $10/hour earns 6 × 5 × $10 = $300 a week.
SalaryAn agreed annual figure, divided by 12 and paid monthly (in the USA often divided by 24 and paid fortnightly). Hours may vary each month, the pay never does.
CommissionA percentage of the value of the sale. Estate agents typically receive 3 to 7% of a property's selling price. It motivates employees to maximise sales.
BonusMoney paid on top of salary, usually a single annual payment, often when the firm makes high profits or as a reward for exceptional performance.
Piece rate payA fixed amount for each completed item, e.g. 25 rupees for every pair of socks a worker produces.
Performance related pay (PRP)Payment based on how well the worker performs. People doing exactly the same job can receive different pay for different outcomes.
Share optionsShares in the company, usually in addition to salary. Their value on any day is number of shares × share price.
weekly wage = hourly wage × hours per day × days per week  ·  value of shares = number of shares × share price
Worked example 1

A worker on a salary of $24,000 a year also holds 800 share options. The share price rises from $3.50 to $5.20 during the year. Calculate her total pay from both sources this year.

  1. Salary is fixed: $24,000 regardless of hours worked
  2. Value of shares at the new price: 800 × $5.20 = $4,160
  3. Total: $24,000 + $4,160 = $28,160

$28,160 ($24,000 salary + $4,160 in shares)

Exam tipWhen a question describes pay that "varies with output" or "is paid per unit", it is a piece rate or commission. When it is "a share of profit paid once a year on top of salary", it is a bonus. Name the factor before you explain it: one mark is usually for the label.

2. Non-wage factors: everything else that matters

Non-wage factors are the influences outside the pay packet that still change whether a worker accepts, stays in or leaves a job. They are the reason two jobs with identical wages are never really identical.

Non-wage factorWhy it influences occupational choice
Fringe benefitsChildcare, free lunches, gym membership or a company car, provided on top of salary. They can be worth thousands and sway a decision on their own.
Length of training or educationThe longer the study or training, the fewer people enter that occupation. It usually takes seven years to qualify as a lawyer.
Job securityContracts differ by industry: some run for one to four years. A long contract (or a permanent post) reduces the risk of losing income.
Job satisfactionEnjoying the work itself is a large part of job satisfaction, and workers will often change career to get more of it.
Career prospectsA defined pathway for promotion, with salary increases attached, makes a job far more attractive.
Level of challengeSome workers choose an occupation for the challenge of the role, e.g. firefighters.
StatusJobs with high recognition in society appeal to many workers: doctors, surgeons and lawyers.
Exam trap"A high wage means everyone will take the job." No. Long training, poor security or low status can repel workers even at high pay, and a low-paid job with great benefits and security can still attract. In "discuss" questions, always weigh both sides of the wage vs non-wage balance.
Model sentence"Although the salary is lower, the generous fringe benefits (on-site childcare) and higher job security mean the non-wage factors outweigh the wage factor for this worker."

3. The demand for labour is a derived demand

The labour market has two sides: households sell labour, firms buy it. Workers supply labour, firms demand labour. Crucially, the demand for labour is a derived demand: it exists only because there is demand for the good or service the labour produces. If demand for the product rises, demand for labour rises, and vice versa.

DeterminantChain of analysis
The price of the product being producedSelling price rises → firm is incentivised to supply more → demand for labour rises.
The demand for the final productBooming economy → demand for most goods and services high → demand for labour high. Recession → demand falls → demand for labour falls.
Ability to substitute capital for labourFirms constantly ask whether machines are cheaper than workers. If switching to capital is more cost effective, demand for labour falls.
The productivity of labourTraining raises productivity → average costs fall → firm expands and demand for labour rises.
Worked example 2

Air travel demand surges after a recession ends. Using the concept of derived demand, analyse what happens to the labour market for airline pilots.

  1. Demand for air travel (the product) rises, so airlines need more flights.
  2. Demand for labour is derived from product demand, so the demand for pilots shifts to the right.
  3. At the old wage there is now excess demand for pilots, so the wage rate rises and the quantity of labour employed rises.
  4. Context: pilot training takes years, so supply barely moves in the short run and most of the adjustment appears as a higher wage.

Derived demand shifts DL right → higher equilibrium wage and higher employment of pilots.

Exam tipAny "explain why demand for X workers changed" question starts with the product: product demand or product price first, labour second. Never begin with the wage.

4. Factors influencing the supply of labour

Supply of labour to an industry is the number of workers willing and able to work in it at each wage. Eight determinants appear in the syllabus. Learn them as chains, not as a list of words.

FactorEffect on supply of labour
Training periodLong, costly training acts as a barrier to entry, excluding many households from those occupations.
Wages in other occupationsComparative wage rates in substitute labour markets pull workers across. It is getting harder to recruit economics teachers because the private sector pays more for their skills.
Migration policyPolicies that raise the net migration rate increase supply to certain industries. In 2022, migrants made up 36% of Singapore's labour force.
Income tax levelsAt a certain level tax becomes a disincentive. As income tax rises, labour supply falls (and vice versa).
Working conditionsGood conditions and non-wage benefits attract workers, e.g. tech firms known for laid-back environments, on-site childcare and restaurants.
Trade union powerUnions can increase supply to an industry when workers value belonging: higher wages and a safer workplace.
Level of welfare benefitsThe higher the benefits, the lower the incentive for low-skilled workers to offer their labour.
Social trendsMajor changes in society shift supply, e.g. working from home during Covid changed the labour market, and not all workers returned when economies reopened.
Exam trapTrade unions are usually written as a wage factor, but in this syllabus union membership sits in supply (workers choose to join) and in bargaining power (section 7). Read what the question is asking for before you place it.

5. The labour market diagram

The labour market is a factor market, and factor markets follow exactly the same rules as product markets: they respond to price, to demand and supply, and to the price elasticity of demand and supply.

  • Equilibrium occurs where demand for labour (DL) equals supply of labour (SL).
  • DL slopes down: firms demand more labour as the wage rate falls.
  • SL slopes up: workers supply more labour as the wage rate rises.
  • Individual firms are price takers: they must accept the industry wage. Offer less and you struggle to recruit; offer more and huge numbers apply.

In the market for graphic designers the equilibrium wage is W and the equilibrium quantity is Q, where DL = SL.

Diagram analysis, copy this structure
  • The market for graphic designers is in equilibrium where DL = SL.
  • The equilibrium wage is We and the quantity of labour is Qe.
  • There is no excess supply of labour and no excess demand for labour at this wage.
Exam tipLabel the axes Wage rate and Quantity of labour, and write DL and SL (not plain D and S). Mark the equilibrium with a point and dashed lines to both axes, then name the wage and quantity.

6. The price elasticity of demand and supply of labour

Factor markets obey the elasticity rules you met for products, applied to the wage rate:

PED of labour = %Δ quantity of labour demanded ÷ %Δ wage rate  ·  PES of labour = %Δ quantity of labour supplied ÷ %Δ wage rate

6.1 Price inelastic demand and supply: NBA players

In 2022 LeBron James received a salary of $45m. Demand for highly skilled players is very price inelastic because clubs want the very best players almost irrespective of what they cost. Supply is also price inelastic: a large rise in the wage would barely change the quantity supplied, because it takes years to develop that level of skill. The equilibrium sits at W1Q1: a high wage and a relatively low quantity of workers.

6.2 Price elastic demand and supply: building site labourers

For unskilled labourers the demand is price elastic: a small drop in wages leads firms to employ many more workers quickly. Supply is price elastic too: because no long training is needed, a small rise in wages brings a large increase in workers willing to do the job. The equilibrium at W1Q1 is a low wage and a relatively high quantity.

Inelastic labour market (skilled)Elastic labour market (unskilled)
DemandClubs hire the best regardless of costFirms respond quickly to a small wage change
SupplyYears of training, few candidatesNo training barrier, many candidates
EquilibriumHigh wage, low quantityLow wage, high quantity
Exam tipThe steepness of the curves is the whole answer. For inelastic labour draw steep DL and SL; for elastic labour draw flat ones, then read off the wage and quantity. See chapter 05 (PED) and chapter 06 (PES) for the general rules.

7. Bargaining power and the minimum wage

7.1 Relative bargaining power

Bargaining power is the worker's ability to negotiate a higher wage. Five factors set it:

  • Trade union membership: unions represent workers in negotiations, and members frequently earn more than non-members.
  • Age and experience: young, inexperienced workers have little power; as workers age, eventually age itself counts against them.
  • Level of education: education brings skill and specialisation, raising power relative to unskilled workers.
  • Current supply conditions: a shortage of willing workers raises power. Before Brexit, hotel workers had little power; the shortage that followed pushed hotel wages up.
  • Government policy: a minimum wage sets a legal floor under negotiations.

7.2 The national minimum wage

Governments intervene to improve equity and avoid the exploitation of workers. A minimum wage is a legally imposed wage level that employers must pay, set above the market rate, and it often varies with the worker's age.

Worked example 3

The market for UK truck drivers is in equilibrium at WeQe. The government imposes a national minimum wage at W1. Analyse the effects using the diagram.

  1. The minimum wage sits above the equilibrium: W1 > We.
  2. Incentivised by the higher wage, the supply of labour rises from Qe to Qs.
  3. Facing higher production costs, the demand for labour falls from Qe to Qd.
  4. At W1 there is now excess supply of labour of QdQs, which is unemployment, because more people want to drive trucks than firms want to hire.

W1 above We → Qs on the supply curve, Qd on the demand curve → excess supply QdQs = unemployment.

Exam trapExcess supply, not excess demand: a minimum wage above equilibrium always pushes quantity supplied above quantity demanded. And a minimum wage set below the equilibrium has no effect at all, because the market wage is already higher. Also, unemployment caused this way grows if demand for that labour is price elastic (firms cut jobs sharply for a small wage rise).
Evaluation pointFor the discuss question: winners are the workers still in jobs (higher wage, more equity), losers are those pushed out of work (Qe to Qd) and firms facing higher costs, who may raise prices. Mention the government price intervention logic from chapter 04.

8. Why pay differs: wage differentials

A wage differential is a difference in pay between workers. Four categories of difference are examined, and for each you must be able to give reasons.

DifferentialReasons
Men and womenMen usually work full-time while women often work part-time to meet childcare demands; women more often have interrupted careers and miss advancement chances; women may accept work below their skill level to fit family needs. The gender pay gap is discrimination: a woman paid less than a man doing exactly the same job.
Industrial sector (primary, secondary, tertiary)Primary sector pay is low: jobs are often unskilled and raw materials earn the lowest profits in the chain. Secondary sector workers add value to raw materials, so products sell for higher profits and wages rise. Tertiary pay is highest: skills take years to acquire and services can be complex and expensive, e.g. artificial intelligence coders.
Private and public sectorSee the comparison below. Private pay is set by markets and profit, public pay is set by government.
Skilled and unskilledUnskilled labour is in high supply, so employers can push wages down (there is always someone willing to work for less). Skill takes time and money to build, so the supply of specific skillsets is limited and those wages are higher.
Private sector workersPublic sector workers
Salaries can be extremely high when the value of output is high and workers are productiveWages reach a maximum ceiling, often below what the private sector offers, e.g. public school teachers earn less than private school teachers
Some salaries are very low as firms cut costs to maximise profits, e.g. garment workers in BangladeshWages rarely fall as low as the worst private sector jobs, because many public workers belong to trade unions
Wage benefits tend to be better: bonuses and share optionsJob security is high, giving long careers with defined promotion pathways
Pay can be volatile with profitsPensions are often very good, though limited compared with the best private pensions
Exam tipLink differentials back to demand and supply for full marks. Skilled workers: restricted supply + high derived demand = high wage. Unskilled workers: huge supply + elastic demand = low wage. That single link usually carries the evaluation mark.
Worked example 4

Explain why a tertiary-sector software engineer earns more than a primary-sector farm labourer.

  1. Supply: the engineer's skills took years to acquire, so the supply of qualified engineers is limited; supply of unskilled farm labour is abundant.
  2. Demand: demand for AI-related services is a derived demand from a high-value product, while farm output generates low profits in the production chain.
  3. Conclusion: limited supply plus high-value derived demand gives a high equilibrium wage for the engineer, and abundant supply plus low-value output gives a low wage for the labourer.

Differentials come from differences in supply (skill, training) and differences in derived demand (value of the product).

9. The division of labour and specialisation

Observing a pin factory, Adam Smith noted that one worker performing all 18 processes (cutting the wire, sharpening the end, stamping the head and so on) made no more than 20 pins a day. Split the tasks and let workers specialise in one, and he estimated 10 workers could produce 48,000 pins a day.

  • Division of labour: a task is broken up into several component tasks.
  • Specialisation: workers focus on one (or a few) components, gaining significant skill in it, which raises output per worker and productivity.
  • It happens at four levels: individual; business (one firm making only drill bits for concrete); regional (Silicon Valley in tech); global (Bangladesh specialising in textiles for export).
StakeholderProsCons
WorkerCan acquire the single skill quickly; gains recognition and status for performing the skill wellWork can be repetitive and boring; limited chance to gain extra skills; if the firm replaces labour with capital, a narrow skill base makes finding new work difficult
FirmTraining time for new workers is short; increased output raises sales and profit; higher productivity lowers cost per unit, making goods more competitive in exportsProductivity can fall through boredom and lower motivation; staff turnover may be high as workers seek interesting jobs elsewhere; firms unable to compete globally may go out of business
EconomyHigher exports cause economic growth, raising income and living standards; export income buys imports, increasing the variety of goods availableOver-dependency on other countries' resources, e.g. Europe's reliance on Russian natural gas during the Ukraine crisis; specialisation accelerates depletion of a country's own resources; entire industries may close, causing structural unemployment
Exam trap"Division of labour always raises productivity" is not a full answer. Boredom, motivation and structural unemployment are the cons, and a discuss question wants both sides weighed with a judgement at the end.
Exam tipThe stakeholder table is your answer plan: workers, firms, economy. In a 6-mark question, give one pro and one con per stakeholder, then judge which outweighs the other in the specific scenario.

11. Practice set and answers

Do every question on paper first. Open an answer only after you have committed to one.

Set A · Wage factors, demand and supply of labour

Define derived demand.
Demand for a factor of production that exists because there is demand for the good or service it helps to produce. The demand for labour depends on the demand for the firm's product.
A salesperson earns a basic salary plus 3% of every sale. Identify the two wage factors involved and explain how the payment structure should affect output.
Salary (fixed annual pay, divided by 12) and commission (3% of the transaction value). The commission gives a direct incentive to maximise sales, so output and revenue per worker rise.
A worker completes 40 items a day and is paid 25 rupees per item. Identify the wage factor and calculate daily pay.
Piece rate pay. 40 × 25 = 1,000 rupees a day.
Explain two factors that would reduce the supply of labour into the teaching profession.
(i) Wages in other occupations: the private sector pays more for the same skills, pulling teachers out (comparative wage rates). (ii) Income tax or long training: higher tax reduces the incentive to work, and long training deters entry. Either chain, clearly explained, earns the marks.
Explain how a recession would affect the labour market for construction workers.
Recession → demand for new buildings and renovation (the product) falls → since demand for labour is derived, DL shifts left → at the old wage there is excess demand for jobs, so the wage rate falls and employment falls.

Set B · Diagrams, differentials and specialisation

The diagram shows a labour market in equilibrium at WeQe. The government sets a minimum wage above We. Analyse the effects on employment and unemployment.
At the minimum wage (W1) supply rises along SL from Qe to Qs while demand falls along DL from Qe to Qd, so employment falls to Qd. The gap between quantity supplied and quantity demanded, QdQs, is excess supply of labour, i.e. unemployment. The size of the job loss is bigger when demand for that labour is price elastic.
Explain why the demand for nurses is likely to be price inelastic while the demand for luxury car washers is price elastic.
Demand for labour is derived. Hospitals cannot substitute machines for much of nursing care and the product (healthcare) has few substitutes, so wages can rise with little fall in quantity demanded (inelastic). Luxury car washing has substitutes (machines, self-service) and discretionary product demand, so a small wage rise cuts quantity demanded sharply (elastic).
Explain why skilled workers generally earn more than unskilled workers.
Becoming skilled takes time and money, so the supply of specific skillsets is limited. Unskilled labour is in high supply, so employers can push wages down. Meanwhile derived demand for skilled output tends to be high-value, so the equilibrium wage for skilled workers is higher.
Discuss whether a national minimum wage always helps workers.
For: workers still employed get a higher wage, equity improves, exploitation falls. Against: employment falls from Qe to Qd, creating unemployment QdQs; firms face higher costs which may be passed into higher prices; only those with inelastic demand keep jobs. Judgement: it helps in low-wage markets with inelastic demand for labour, but harms those priced out of work.
Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of specialisation for a firm.
Advantages: short training time, higher output, higher productivity lowering cost per unit and boosting export competitiveness. Disadvantages: boredom lowers motivation and productivity, high staff turnover, and firms that cannot compete globally may close. Judgement: the balance depends on whether productivity gains outweigh turnover and whether demand for the specialised product holds up.

  • Opened every labour demand answer with derived demand and the product before the wage.
