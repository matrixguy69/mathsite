Chapter 01 · Economics

Demand: quantity demanded, movements and shifts

The full demand chapter for CAIE 2281 and IGCSE 0455: the definition, adding up individual demand, extension versus increase, every condition of demand, and the past-paper questions worked through with model answers.

CAIE 2281 IGCSE 0455

1. What is demand?

Demand is the amount of a good or service that a consumer is willing and able to purchase at a given price in a given time period. Two words carry all the marks: willing (they want it) and able (they can afford it).

  • Want alone is not demand. You may want a yacht; unless you can pay for it at today's price, you add nothing to market demand.
  • Demand is always for a time period: so many tonnes per week, so many litres per day. Without a time period the number means nothing.
  • The law of demand: price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions. Price up, quantity demanded down; price down, quantity demanded up (other things being equal, ceteris paribus).
  • A demand curve is the graphical representation of the price and quantity demanded (QD) by consumers. It slopes downwards from left to right, showing that inverse relationship.
Exam trapNever write "demand rises when the price falls". A change in the price of the good itself changes quantity demanded, shown by a movement along the curve. "Demand" rises only when something other than price changes, which shifts the whole curve. This distinction is worth its own mark every year.

2. Individual and market demand

Individual demand is what one consumer will buy at each price. Market demand is the combination of all the individual demand for a good or service. It is calculated by adding up the individual quantities demanded at each price level.

market demand at price P = Σ (individual quantity demanded at P)

Build the market column price by price, never by adding the totals at the bottom.

Using the schedule above, find market demand at $4 and explain what happens to market demand as price rises.

Exam tipIf a question shows a table with only two consumers, the market demand column is free marks: add across each row and label the column "market demand". State that you are adding quantities at each price, because that phrase is the definition.

3. Movements along the demand curve

If price is the only factor that changes (ceteris paribus), there is a change in the quantity demanded (QD). This is shown by a movement along the demand curve. Two names, two directions:

Extension of demand (increase in quantity demanded)fallsrisesmovement down along the curve

Worked example 2

5(a) – Identify how the effect of a fall in the price of gold would be shown on a demand curve for gold.

  1. Only the price of gold has changed, so it is a movement, not a shift.
  2. The price falls, so quantity demanded rises.
  3. On the diagram: move from a point higher up the curve down to a point lower on the same curve, labelling P1, P2, Q1, Q2.

A movement down along the demand curve for gold (an extension of demand), from a higher price and quantity to a lower price and higher quantity.

Exam tipAlways say which way the movement goes. "Movement along the curve" on its own can lose the direction mark: price fell, so quantity demanded rose, so the movement is downwards along the curve.

4. Conditions of demand (shifts of the curve)

There are numerous factors that will change the demand for a good or service irrespective of the price level. Collectively these factors are called the conditions of demand. A change in any of them shifts the entire demand curve, as opposed to a movement along it.

Worked example 3

Explain what happens in the market for bus transport when petrol prices rise sharply. Use a chain of analysis.

  1. Petrol is a complement to car travel, so driving becomes dearer and buses become relatively cheaper.
  2. The demand curve for bus transport shifts right from D1 to D2 (Figure 2).
  3. At the old fare, quantity demanded now exceeds quantity supplied: there is excess demand.
  4. Bus operators raise the fare (or add more services), so price rises.
  5. As price rises, quantity demanded contracts along D2 and quantity supplied extends along the (unchanged) supply curve.
  6. A new equilibrium is reached.

The demand curve shifts right, creating excess demand at the original fare; the fare and the number of journeys both rise at the new equilibrium.

Shift or movement, the two-line testAsk yourself: did the price of this good itself change? Yes, it is a movement along the curve (extension or contraction). No, it is a shift of the whole curve (increase or decrease in demand), and you must name the condition of demand that caused it.

5. Substitutes, complements and income

Three pairs of relationships appear in almost every demand question. Learn the direction of the effect, not just the definition.

TermDefinitionExample
SubstituteA good used in place of another. Price of X up, demand for its substitute up.tea and coffee, bus and train, butter and margarine
ComplementA good used together with another. Price of X up, demand for its complement down.cars and petrol, printers and ink cartridges, tennis racquets and tennis balls
Normal goodDemand rises as income rises.holidays, restaurant meals
Inferior goodDemand falls as income rises.instant noodles, second-hand clothes, bus travel in some cities
Worked example 4

The price of coffee rises sharply. Analyse the effect on the market for tea and the market for sugar.

  1. Coffee and tea are substitutes, so consumers switch: the demand curve for tea shifts right.
  2. At the old price of tea there is excess demand, so the price of tea rises and quantity supplied extends to a new, higher equilibrium.
  3. Coffee and sugar are complements, so less coffee means less sugar: the demand curve for sugar shifts left.
  4. At the old price of sugar there is excess supply, so its price falls to a new, lower equilibrium.

Demand for tea rises (higher price and quantity), while demand for sugar falls (lower price and quantity).

Exam trap"The price of tea falls, so demand for tea rises" is wrong wording. A fall in the price of tea causes an extension in the quantity demanded of tea, a movement along its own curve. Demand for tea changes only when income, tastes or the price of coffee or sugar changes.
Exam tipFor a define-and-example question, give the definition first, then the direction of the effect, then one named pair: "A substitute is a good used in place of another; when the price of tea rises, the demand for coffee increases, for example tea and coffee."

6. Why demand differs between countries and groups

The same product can have very different levels of demand in different countries or between income groups. Every one of these differences is a condition of demand, so the answer is always a shift of the curve explained in context.

InfluenceHow it changes demand
Income levelsHigher incomes raise demand for normal and luxury goods; demand for inferior goods may fall.
Tastes, culture, climate, religionA colder country demands more heating fuel; dietary rules and fashion change what people buy.
Population and age structureMore people, or more children, means more buyers at every price.
Distribution of incomeA country with a large middle class demands more mid-priced goods than one where income is concentrated.
Prices of related goodsIf substitutes are dearer abroad, demand for the local product is higher.
Advertising and informationHeavy promotion raises tastes and therefore demand.
Worked example 5

Winter coats sell far better in Canada than in Nigeria. Explain why, using demand analysis.

  1. Climate and tastes differ: in Canada a warm coat is a necessity, so preferences shift demand right.
  2. Income and population differences add to it: more households able to buy at each price.
  3. At the same price, quantity demanded in Canada is much higher, so market demand is higher.

Different conditions of demand (climate, tastes and incomes) mean the demand curve for winter coats sits far to the right in Canada, so more is demanded at every price.

Exam tipWhen a question says "one country than another" or "one group of consumers", reach for the big three first: income, tastes, population. Then add prices of related goods or distribution of income for the fourth point.

7. Practice set and answers

Do every question on paper first. Open an answer only after you have committed to one.

Set A · Definitions and movements

Q3(b)/22/O/N/20(b) Explain the difference between an extension in demand and an increase in demand.
An extension in demand is a movement down along the existing demand curve, caused only by a fall in the price of the good itself, so quantity demanded rises. An increase in demand is a rightward shift of the whole curve, caused by a change in a condition of demand (income, tastes, the price of a substitute or complement), so more is demanded at every price. Extension: price changes, curve stays. Increase: price unchanged, curve moves.
5(a) – Identify how the effect of a fall in the price of gold would be shown on a demand curve for gold.
As a movement down along the demand curve for gold, from a higher point to a lower point on the same curve (an extension of demand). Quantity demanded rises. The curve does not shift.
Q4(a)/22/M/J/18(a) Define a substitute and give an example.
A substitute is a good that is used in place of another good. When the price of one rises, the demand for the other increases (its curve shifts right). Example: tea and coffee (also bus and train travel, or butter and margarine).
2281/Q3(b)/23/O/N/24(b) Explain the relationship between:(i) demand for tennis racquets and demand for badminton racquets(ii) demand for tennis racquets and demand for tennis balls.
(i) They are substitutes: they compete for the same leisure time and spending, so if demand for tennis racquets rises, demand for badminton racquets falls. (ii) They are complements: they are used together, so if demand for tennis racquets rises, demand for tennis balls also rises.
Define demand and explain why a wish to buy a sports car is not demand.
Demand is the amount a consumer is willing and able to purchase at a given price in a given time period. A wish alone shows willingness but not ability to pay, so nothing is added to demand until the consumer can afford the car at its price.

Set B · Causes of changes in demand

Q7(b)/21/M/J/17(b) Explain two causes of an increase in demand for bus transport.
(1) A rise in the price of substitutes such as train fares or petrol for car travel makes buses cheaper by comparison, so the demand curve for bus transport shifts right. (2) A growth in the population of a town, a successful advertising campaign, or a government congestion charge on cars increases the number of people wanting to travel by bus at every fare.
Q5(b)/23/O/N/18(b) Explain two influences on a country’s demand for food.
(1) Income: as incomes rise, households buy more food and shift towards better quality and luxury food, so demand for normal and premium food rises. (2) Tastes and preferences: health trends, religion, culture and climate change what people want to eat, shifting demand towards some foods and away from others. (Also acceptable: population size, distribution of income, prices of restaurant meals versus home cooking, advertising.)
Q2(b)/21/M/J/21(b) Explain two causes of a shift to the right in the demand curve for fish.
(1) A rise in the price of substitutes such as chicken or beef makes fish cheaper by comparison, so consumers switch to fish. (2) A change in tastes, for example a successful campaign on the health benefits of fish, or a rise in incomes or population, increases demand for fish at every price. Both shift the whole curve right.
Q1(b)/22/O/N/22(b) Identify two ways a bank could increase demand for its services.
(1) An advertising campaign to improve the bank's image and raise the popularity of its accounts. (2) Launching a new service people want, such as a better mobile banking app or a rewards account, which changes tastes and shifts demand right. Note: cutting the bank's own charges would be a fall in price, which is an extension in demand, not an increase in demand.
2(b) – Explain two reasons why someone may switch from eating in one restaurant to eating in another restaurant.
(1) A change in relative prices: the first restaurant raises its prices or the second cuts them, so the second becomes cheaper by comparison and demand for it shifts right. (2) A change in tastes and quality: better reviews, a new menu, advertising or a more convenient location makes the second restaurant more popular, shifting demand for it to the right.
Q2(b)/21/M/J/17(b) Explain two causes of a shift of a supply curve to the right.
(1) An improvement in technology that lowers production costs, so more is supplied at every price. (2) A government subsidy to producers (or a fall in the costs of raw materials, or new firms entering the market), which also raises supply at every price. Both are conditions of supply: see Chapter 02 · Supply.
Q2(c)/21/M/J/17(c) Analyse why the price of a product may be higher in the USA than China.
Each country is a separate market with its own demand and supply, so each settles at its own equilibrium price. In the USA, demand may be higher (higher incomes, stronger tastes for the product, larger advertising) which shifts demand right and raises price. Alternatively, supply may be lower (higher wages and rents, stricter regulation and taxes, higher transport costs), shifting supply left and raising price. Either way the equilibrium of demand and supply settles at a higher price than in China.

Set C · Analysis and diagrams

Q2(b)/22/M/J/19(c) Analyse, using a demand and supply diagram, the effect of an increase in demand for cars on the market for tyres.
Cars and tyres are complements. The increase in demand for cars shifts the demand curve for cars right, so more cars are bought. Each car needs four tyres, so at every price the demand curve for tyres shifts right along the unchanged supply curve of tyres. At the original tyre price there is now excess demand, so the price of tyres rises. As price rises, quantity demanded of tyres contracts along the new demand curve and quantity supplied extends along the supply curve. Result: a new equilibrium with a higher price and a higher quantity of tyres traded.
Q5(c)/22/O/N/19(c) Analyse why the demand for a product may be higher in one country than in another country.
Because the conditions of demand differ. (1) Incomes: higher average incomes mean consumers are more able to buy, shifting demand for normal goods right. (2) Tastes, climate and culture: a colder country demands more heating fuel; dietary and cultural habits change what is bought. (3) Population and its age structure gives more buyers at each price. (4) Prices of related goods differ, changing the demand for substitutes and complements. Any of these shifts the demand curve right, so at the same price more is demanded in that country.
A government cuts the price of school meals. Is this an increase in demand or an extension in demand? Explain how you would show it on a diagram.
It is an extension in demand (a rise in quantity demanded): the price of the meal itself has fallen, so you move down along the existing demand curve from one point to another, labelling the lower price and the higher quantity. The curve does not shift.
Explain what happens to the demand curve for instant noodles when average incomes in a country rise.
Instant noodles are an inferior good, so higher income reduces demand: the demand curve shifts to the left. At every price fewer noodles are demanded, and at the old price there is excess supply, pushing the price down to a new equilibrium with a lower quantity.

9. Exam checklist

  • Definition includes willing and able, at a given price and in a given time period.
  • Market demand built by adding individual quantities at each price, not by adding totals.
  • Price of the good itself changed? Then it is a movement (extension down, contraction up), never a shift.
  • Any other factor? Then it is a shift, and you have named the condition of demand behind it.
  • Direction always stated: "shifts right, demand increases" or "shifts left, demand falls".
  • Substitutes (price up, demand for rival up) and complements (price up, demand for partner down) identified correctly.
  • Normal and inferior goods linked to income direction.
  • Diagram questions written as a chain: shift the curve, spot the excess demand or supply, move the price, finish with the new equilibrium in context.
