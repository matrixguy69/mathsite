Chapter 06 · Economics

Price elasticity of supply: how quickly can producers respond?

The mirror of PED: the definition and formula of PES, its full range from zero to infinity, the five determinants (time, spare capacity, stocks, perishability, nature of the product) and what elasticity means for producers and consumers when markets are disturbed.

CAIE 2281 IGCSE 0455

1. What PES measures

The law of supply says that when price rises, quantity supplied rises (a movement up along the supply curve). Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how responsive quantity supplied is to a change in price.

  • It is always positive, because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction.
  • It answers a practical question: if the market price of your product rises, how quickly and how far can you deliver more of it?
  • Responsiveness differs hugely between products: a farmer can send more eggs to market tomorrow; a housebuilder cannot conjure new flats overnight.
PED vs PES in one breathPED is about buyers switching away when price moves. PES is about sellers being able to move more output in when price moves. Same maths, different side of the market.

2. Calculating PES

PES = % change in quantity supplied% change in price = %ΔQS%ΔP
% change = new value − original valueoriginal value × 100
Worked example 1

The price of eggs rises by 20% and farmers supply 5% more eggs. Calculate PES.

  1. %ΔP = 20%
  2. %ΔQS = +5%
  3. PES = 5 / 20 = 0.25
  4. Below 1, so supply is price-inelastic: farmers react only slightly.

PES = 0.25 (price-inelastic)

Worked example 2

A T-shirt firm can raise its price by 10% and increase output by 30% within a month by adding a shift. Calculate PES.

  1. %ΔP = 10%
  2. %ΔQS = +30%
  3. PES = 30 / 10 = 3
  4. Above 1, so supply is price-elastic: output responds more than proportion to price.

PES = 3 (price-elastic)

Exam tipSame method as PED, but remember PES is positive: no absolute-value gymnastics needed. Show the percentage working for method marks.

3. The values of PES

PES runs from zero to infinity. Learn what each end of the range means and one good example of each.

TypeValueWhat it meansExamples
Perfectly inelastic0Quantity supplied does not respond to price at allShort-run supply of land in a fixed location; a one-off painting by a dead artist
Relatively inelastic0 to 1%ΔQS is smaller than %ΔPAgriculture in the short run (crops take a season); housing while land and planning are scarce
Unit elastic1%ΔQS equals %ΔPThe textbook boundary case, rare in practice
Relatively elasticgreater than 1%ΔQS is larger than %ΔPManufacturing with spare capacity; services that can add staff quickly
Perfectly elasticinfinityAt the market price firms will supply any amount; a hair below it and quantity supplied falls to zeroTheoretical; a single firm in perfect competition
Exam trap"Supply of housing is perfectly inelastic" is too strong for real markets. Say "highly inelastic in the short run" and explain why (land, planning, build time). Reserve "zero" and "infinity" for the theoretical cases the question explicitly asks about.

4. The five determinants of PES

PES is decided by how easily a firm can change its output when price moves. Five factors cover every exam question.

DeterminantMore elastic when...WhyExam example
TimeMore time is allowed to adjustFirms can hire, build, plant and expand only graduallyFresh eggs respond in days; new mines take years. PES rises as the time period lengthens
Spare capacityThe firm has unused machinery, buildings or labourExtra output can come from idle resources at little extra costA factory running one shift can add a second: elastic. A factory at full capacity: inelastic until new capital is bought
StocksFinished goods are stored easilyStocks can be released immediately when price risesCanned food and metals: elastic. Services like haircuts cannot be stored at all
PerishabilityThe product keeps for a long timeNon-perishable goods can be held back or released at willFresh fish and cut flowers: must be sold now, so short-run supply is inelastic and even backward-bending; tinned fish: elastic
Nature of the productIt is manufactured from standard inputsFactory output can be scaled up; farming and natural products cannotCar assembly: relatively elastic. Farm produce: inelastic short run (weather, biology); services and unique goods: inelastic
Worked example 3

Explain why the supply of fresh strawberries is price-inelastic in the short run but more elastic in the long run. (own question in syllabus style)

  1. Strawberries are perishable and take a growing season, so within a season farmers cannot produce more just because price rose: short-run PES is low.
  2. Given several seasons, farmers can plant more bushes, buy more land and adopt better techniques, so quantity supplied responds strongly to sustained high prices.
  3. Therefore PES is inelastic in the short run and rises towards elastic as the time period lengthens.

Short run: perishability and growing time make PES inelastic. Long run: new plantings and land make PES more elastic.

Worked example 4

Explain how spare capacity affects the price elasticity of supply of a car factory. (own question in syllabus style)

  1. If the factory is running well below maximum output, a higher car price lets it add shifts and use idle machines.
  2. Output can rise by a large % for a small % price rise, so PES is high (elastic).
  3. If the factory is already at full capacity, only overtime or new capital can raise output, so PES is low (inelastic) until investment happens.

Spare capacity makes supply elastic; no spare capacity makes it inelastic until new capacity is built.

5. Drawing elastic and inelastic supply

The shape of the supply curve shows its elasticity on a given set of axes: flat means elastic, steep means inelastic.

Exam tipIn "analyse with a diagram" questions about time periods, you can draw two supply curves on one set of axes: S1 steep (short run) and S2 flatter (long run), then show that the same demand shift produces a bigger quantity response on S2. Label which curve is which time period.

6. Who is affected: producers and consumers

When demand changes, PES decides who carries the price change. This is one of the most examiner-friendly links in the whole syllabus.

SituationEffect on priceEffect on quantityWho gains, who loses
Demand rises, supply elasticPrice rises only a littleQuantity rises a lotConsumers cope well (small price hike); producers sell much more volume
Demand rises, supply inelasticPrice rises a lotQuantity rises only a littleConsumers suffer: they bid against each other; the lucky producers of the scarce units gain
Demand falls, supply elasticPrice falls only a littleQuantity falls a lotProducers lose heavily on volume; consumers pay almost the old price
Demand falls, supply inelasticPrice falls a lotQuantity falls only a littleProducers suffer a big price drop; consumers enjoy the bargain
Worked example 5

A harvest failure reduces the supply of tomatoes. Explain who is most affected: consumers or farmers, given that the supply of tomatoes is highly inelastic in the short run. (own question in syllabus style)

  1. A supply shift left against a given demand curve raises the equilibrium price and cuts quantity.
  2. Because short-run supply is inelastic (perishable crop, next harvest months away), the leftward shift produces a large price rise and only a small fall in quantity traded.
  3. Consumers pay much more for tomatoes and most of the loss hits them; the farmers who still have a crop receive a much higher price per unit.
  4. Over the long run, farmers plant more and supply becomes more elastic, so the price falls back towards normal.

Inelastic short-run supply means consumers bear most of a harvest shock through sharply higher prices.

Worked example 6

The price of a commodity rises because of a surge in world demand. Explain why the price rise is larger when supply is inelastic. (own question in syllabus style)

  1. A demand shift moves the market along the supply curve to the new equilibrium.
  2. On a steep (inelastic) supply curve, the same horizontal demand shift translates mostly into price, barely into quantity.
  3. On a flat (elastic) supply curve, the same shift translates mostly into quantity, only slightly into price.
  4. So consumers of inelastic-supply goods face the worst price spikes, as with housing in land-scarce cities or oil in the short run.

With inelastic supply the market cannot expand output, so the demand surge appears almost entirely as a higher price.

Producer side, one summaryProducers love elastic supply when demand is rising (they sell volume at roughly the old price). They suffer when their own supply is inelastic and demand falls: they cannot cut output quickly, so price crashes on what they cannot sell. Time is the escape: PES rises as adjustment periods lengthen.

7. Practice set and answers

Do every question on paper first. Open an answer only after you have committed to one.

Set A · Definition, calculation and values

Define price elasticity of supply and state its formula. (own question)
PES measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price. PES = %ΔQS ÷ %ΔP. Its value is always zero or positive.
The world price of rubber rises by 25% and plantations increase sales by 10%. Calculate PES and classify it. (own question)
PES = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4. Below 1, so supply is price-inelastic: trees take years to mature, so output barely responds.
A bakery raises prices by 8% and increases production by 24%. Calculate PES and classify it. (own question)
PES = 24 ÷ 8 = 3. Above 1, so supply is price-elastic: the bakery has spare ovens and can buy flour immediately.
Explain what is meant by perfectly inelastic supply and give one example. (own question)
PES = 0: quantity supplied does not change at all when price changes. Example: a fixed plot of land in a city centre, or a unique painting by a dead artist, whatever the price offered.
State the full range of values for price elasticity of supply. (own question)
From 0 (perfectly inelastic) through values between 0 and 1 (relatively inelastic), 1 (unit elastic) and above 1 (relatively elastic), up to infinity (perfectly elastic). Unlike PED, PES never goes negative.

Set B · Determinants

Explain two factors that determine the price elasticity of supply of a product. (own question in the style of 2281)
(1) Time: firms need time to hire, build and plant, so PES rises as the adjustment period lengthens. (2) Spare capacity: with idle machines and labour, output can expand quickly (elastic); at full capacity, supply is inelastic until new capital is installed. Alternatives: stocks, perishability, nature of the product.
Explain why the supply of fresh fish is more price-inelastic than the supply of frozen fish. (own question)
Fresh fish is highly perishable: it must be sold within days, and catches cannot be stored to wait for better prices. Frozen fish can be stockpiled and released when price rises, so quantity supplied responds much more: higher PES.
Explain how the price elasticity of supply of housing changes over time. (own question)
In the short run the stock of houses is almost fixed (land, planning, build time), so PES is very low: higher prices cannot create homes quickly. Over years, new construction and conversions raise quantity supplied strongly, so PES becomes much higher.
Explain why the supply of printed T-shirts is more elastic than the supply of oil. (own question)
T-shirts are manufactured from standard inputs with spare capacity and easy fabrics: firms can scale output up quickly, so PES is high. Oil requires drilling, rigs and years of investment, so a price rise brings only a small extra quantity in the short run: PES is low.
"Services have lower PES than manufactured goods." Explain why. (own question)
Services cannot be stored as stock (you cannot bottle a haircut), and extra output usually needs more trained workers hired and trained. Manufactured goods can be produced in batches, stored, and expanded using existing machinery, so PES is higher.

Set C · Effects and diagram work

Draw a demand and supply diagram showing the effect of an increase in demand when supply is price-inelastic. Explain the effect on price and quantity. (own question)
Draw D and a steep S curve, then D2 shifted right. The new equilibrium has a large rise in price and only a small rise in quantity. Because firms cannot expand output quickly, buyers bid up the price of the limited stock.
Explain why a surge in demand for tickets to a one-night concert causes a huge price rise. (own question)
The seat capacity is fixed on the night, so supply is perfectly (or almost perfectly) inelastic. A rightward demand shift therefore appears almost entirely as a higher price, with no extra seats. This is why ticket prices on resale markets explode.
Explain the likely effect on consumers of a rise in demand for strawberries when the supply of strawberries is elastic. (own question)
With elastic supply, producers expand output in response to the higher demand, so the equilibrium quantity rises substantially and the price rises only slightly. Consumers are barely worse off per unit and get more strawberries overall.
Discuss whether a farmer benefits more from a rise in demand when the supply of the crop is elastic or inelastic. (own question)
With inelastic supply the price soars but quantity sold barely changes, so farm revenue usually jumps. With elastic supply, quantity sold expands a lot but price rises little, so revenue rises less. Inelastic supply benefits the farmer more from a demand surge, but the same inelasticity hurts farmers when demand falls.
A government subsidises housing construction. Explain how this changes the supply of housing and who gains. (own question)
The subsidy cuts builders' costs, shifting supply right. A rightward supply shift lowers the equilibrium price and raises quantity: consumers gain from cheaper housing and more units available. Builders gain too through higher sales volume at manageable costs, funded partly by the taxpayer.

8. Quick quiz

Click an option to check your answer.

MCQ 1 Price rises by 15% and quantity supplied rises by 45%. PES is:

Answer: C. PES = 45 ÷ 15 = 3. PES is positive, and a value above 1 means quantity supplied responds more than proportion to price.

MCQ 2 Which product has the most price-inelastic supply in the short run?

Answer: A. Perishable products cannot be stored or expanded quickly, so quantity supplied barely responds to price in the short run.

MCQ 3 Demand for a product rises. If its supply is perfectly inelastic, the result is:

Answer: B. Quantity supplied is fixed, so the demand shift is absorbed entirely by price, such as concert seats on the night.

MCQ 4 Which factor always makes supply more elastic?

Answer: D. Given more time, firms can hire, build and plant, so quantity supplied responds more to price. Shorter time, perishability and no spare capacity all reduce elasticity.

MCQ 5 Demand for housing in a land-scarce city rises. Because supply is inelastic, the main effect is:

Answer: A. Builders cannot add homes quickly, so the extra demand appears mostly as higher prices rather than more houses.

9. Exam checklist

  • Defined PES as the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.
  • Used %ΔQS ÷ %ΔP with the original value as the base, and kept PES positive.
  • Quoted the full range of PES: 0 through inelastic, unit, elastic, up to infinity.
  • Named the determinant (time, spare capacity, stocks, perishability, nature of product) with a one-line link to responsiveness.
  • Argued that PES rises as the time period lengthens whenever a question mentions short run versus long run.
  • Drawn steep S for inelastic and flat S for elastic, with axes, labels and equilibrium points marked.
  • Linked inelastic supply to large price changes and elastic supply to large quantity changes when demand shifts.
  • Said who gains and loses: consumers bear the price spike when supply is inelastic; producers suffer when demand falls against inelastic supply.
  • Used context language (crop seasons, build times, stockrooms) instead of bare "PES is low".
