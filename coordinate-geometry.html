Chapter 04 · Coordinate Geometry

Distance, gradient and the equation of a line

Everything in the 43-minute Zainematics coordinate geometry lesson, worked in full, for Cambridge O Level 4024 and IGCSE 0580: plotting points, distance, midpoint, gradient, y = mx + c, parallel and perpendicular lines, and finding where two lines cross.

1. The coordinate plane

Every point on a flat surface is located by a pair of numbers. Two number lines cross at right angles to make the coordinate plane:

Part of the planeWhat it is
x-axisThe horizontal number line. It tells you how far across to go.
y-axisThe vertical number line. It tells you how far up to go.
OriginThe point where the axes cross, written (0, 0).
PointWritten (x, y): always across first, then up.

Every point splits the plane into four quadrants, named anticlockwise from the top right:

QuadrantSigns of (x, y)Example point
Quadrant I (top right)(+, +)(3, 4)
Quadrant II (top left)(−, +)(−3, 4)
Quadrant III (bottom left)(−, −)(−3, −4)
Quadrant IV (bottom right)(+, −)(3, −4)

1.1 Horizontal and vertical lines

Special lines have equations you should recognise instantly:

  • Horizontal line: every point on it has the same y-value, so its equation is y = constant. Its gradient is 0. The line through (0, 3) and (5, 3) is y = 3.
  • Vertical line: every point on it has the same x-value, so its equation is x = constant. It has no gradient (the gradient is undefined). The line through (−2, 0) and (−2, 7) is x = −2.
Worked example 1

Plot and name the points A(3, 2), B(−4, 5), C(0, −3) and D(−1, −6). Which quadrant is each point in?

  1. A(3, 2): 3 across (right), 2 up. Both positive, so quadrant I.
  2. B(−4, 5): 4 across (left), 5 up. x negative, y positive, so quadrant II.
  3. C(0, −3): x is 0, so it sits on the y-axis, 3 down. It is not inside any quadrant.
  4. D(−1, −6): 1 left, 6 down. Both negative, so quadrant III.

A: quadrant I · B: quadrant II · C: on the y-axis · D: quadrant III

Exam trapPoints with a zero coordinate sit on an axis, not in a quadrant. Also, the order is always across then up: (3, 2) is not the same point as (2, 3).

2. Distance between two points

Take any two points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂). The horizontal gap between them is x₂ − x₁ and the vertical gap is y₂ − y₁. These two gaps are the legs of a right-angled triangle with the line PQ as its hypotenuse, so the distance formula is just Pythagoras applied to those gaps.

distance = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

Subtract in any order you like: squaring kills the sign, so (x₂ − x₁)² is the same as (x₁ − x₂)². Just make sure you pair the x-values together and the y-values together.

Worked example 2

Find the distance between A(1, 3) and B(4, 5).

  1. Horizontal gap: 4 − 1 = 3
  2. Vertical gap: 5 − 3 = 2
  3. Apply Pythagoras: d = √(3² + 2²) = √(9 + 4) = √13

Distance = √13 ≈ 3.61 units

Worked example 3

Find the distance between P(−2, −3) and Q(4, 5).

  1. Horizontal gap: 4 − (−2) = 6
  2. Vertical gap: 5 − (−3) = 8
  3. d = √(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100

Distance = 10 units

Exam tipThe gaps 6 and 8 are a scaled-up 3-4-5 triangle, which is why the answer is a whole number. If the gaps are 3 and 4, the distance is 5; if they are 9 and 12, it is 15. Keep the common triples handy to save calculator time.
Exam trapDo not stop at √100. Write the final distance as a number (10), and give units ("10 units" or "10 cm") if the question provides them.

3. Midpoint of a line segment

The midpoint is the point exactly halfway along the segment joining (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂). It is simply the average of each coordinate: add the two x-values and halve, add the two y-values and halve.

midpoint = x₁ + x₂2, y₁ + y₂2
Worked example 4

Find the midpoint of the segment joining A(2, 7) and B(6, 3).

  1. Average the x-values: (2 + 6) / 2 = 8 / 2 = 4
  2. Average the y-values: (7 + 3) / 2 = 10 / 2 = 5
  3. Write the pair as a point

Midpoint = (4, 5)

Why it worksAveraging the x-values slides you halfway across, and averaging the y-values slides you halfway up. You can check the answer by working the distance from the midpoint to each end: it must be equal on both sides.
Exam trapMidpoint is a point, so the answer needs brackets and a comma: (4, 5). A single number is only ever the answer to a distance question.

4. Gradient

The gradient m measures how steep a line is, and which way it slopes. It is the steepness of the line: how many units the y-value changes for every 1 unit you move to the right.

m = y₂ − y₁x₂ − x₁ = rise / run

Rise over run: between any two points on the line, draw a right-angled triangle. The rise is the vertical side (change in y), the run is the horizontal side (change in x, always measured left to right). Gradient = rise ÷ run.

GradientWhat the line looks likeExample
Positive (m > 0)Uphill, rising left to righty = 2x + 1
Negative (m < 0)Downhill, falling left to righty = −3x + 4
Zero (m = 0)Flat, horizontal liney = 7
Undefined (no gradient)Vertical line, climbs infinitely steepx = 5
Worked example 5

Find the gradient of the line through A(1, 2) and B(4, 8).

  1. Rise (change in y): 8 − 2 = 6
  2. Run (change in x): 4 − 1 = 3
  3. Divide: m = 6 / 3 = 2

Gradient m = 2 (positive, so the line goes uphill)

Worked example 6

Find the gradient of the line through C(0, 5) and D(4, −3).

  1. Rise: −3 − 5 = −8
  2. Run: 4 − 0 = 4
  3. Divide: m = −8 / 4 = −2

Gradient m = −2 (negative, so the line goes downhill)

Exam tipAlways write the gradient as a number: m = −2. If a question asks you to "state the gradient", the mark scheme wants the value, not a word like "steep" or "downhill". Negative gradients still have a number: −2, not 2.
Exam trapThe gradient is not x₂ − x₁ over y₂ − y₁. Y goes on top. Swap them and you get the reciprocal, which is only correct when the gradient happens to be 1 or −1.

5. The equation y = mx + c

Every straight line can be written in gradient–intercept form:

y = mx + c
LetterStands forWhere you read it
mGradientThe number multiplying x
cy-interceptThe number on its own; where the line crosses the y-axis

Read both straight off the equation. For y = 4x − 7: gradient m = 4, y-intercept c = −7, so the line crosses the y-axis at (0, −7).

Exam trapIf the x term is negative, rewrite the equation before reading anything off. For y = 5 − 2x, rearrange to y = −2x + 5: now m = −2 and c = 5. Written the other way round, it is very easy to grab the wrong signs.

5.1 Sketching from an equation

To sketch y = mx + c you only need two points:

  1. Plot the y-intercept (0, c) first.
  2. From that point, use the gradient as rise over run: go 1 unit right, then m units up (or down if m is negative). Mark the second point.
  3. Join the two points with a straight ruler line and extend it.
Worked example 7

Find the gradient and y-intercept of y = 5 − 2x, then describe how to sketch it.

  1. Rearrange: y = −2x + 5
  2. Read off: gradient m = −2, y-intercept c = 5, so it crosses at (0, 5)
  3. From (0, 5), run 1 right and go 2 down to reach (1, 3). Check: −2(1) + 5 = 3 ✓
  4. Join (0, 5) and (1, 3) and extend. The line falls left to right, matching the negative gradient.

m = −2, c = 5. Crosses the y-axis at (0, 5) and falls 2 units for every 1 unit right.

Exam tipWith a whole number gradient like 3, a convenient second point is 1 across and 3 up. With a fractional gradient like 3/2, go 2 across and 3 up so you avoid landing between grid lines.

6. Equation of a line from a gradient and a point

Sometimes the gradient is given but the intercept is not. Use the point–gradient form, where (x₁, y₁) is any known point on the line:

y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)

Substitute the gradient and the point, then expand and rearrange into y = mx + c. Watch the minus signs: if the point is (2, −3), then y₁ = −3, so you write y − (−3), which is y + 3.

Worked example 8

Find the equation of the line with gradient 5 that passes through (2, 3). Give your answer in the form y = mx + c.

  1. Substitute into y − y₁ = m(x − x₁): y − 3 = 5(x − 2)
  2. Expand the bracket: y − 3 = 5x − 10
  3. Add 3 to both sides: y = 5x − 7
  4. Check with the point: 5(2) − 7 = 3 ✓

y = 5x − 7

6.1 Equation from two points

When you are only given two points on the line, build it in two stages:

  1. Find the gradient first using m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁).
  2. Substitute one point (either one, plus the gradient) into y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) and expand.
Worked example 9

Find the equation of the line through A(1, 2) and B(3, 8).

  1. Gradient: m = (8 − 2) / (3 − 1) = 6 / 2 = 3
  2. Use point A: y − 2 = 3(x − 1)
  3. Expand: y − 2 = 3x − 3
  4. Rearrange: y = 3x − 1
  5. Check with point B: 3(3) − 1 = 8 ✓

y = 3x − 1

Exam tipAlways check your finished equation by substituting the other point (the one you did not use). If it fails, your gradient or your expansion is wrong, and you have caught it before the examiner did.
Exam trapThe question says "give your answer in the form y = mx + c". Leaving it as y − 2 = 3(x − 1) loses the mark even though the working is correct. Expand and simplify every time.

7. Parallel lines

Parallel lines never meet, which means they must climb at exactly the same rate. Their gradients are equal.

lines are parallel if m₁ = m₂

For example, y = 2x + 1, y = 2x − 5 and y = 2x are all parallel: same gradient 2, different y-intercepts (three different rungs on the same ladder).

To write the equation of a line parallel to a given line through a given point:

  1. Read or find the gradient of the given line. That is your gradient too.
  2. Substitute that gradient and the given point into y − y₁ = m(x − x₁).
  3. Expand and rearrange to y = mx + c.
Worked example 10

Find the equation of the line parallel to y = 2x + 3 that passes through (4, 5).

  1. Gradient of the given line is m = 2, so our line also has m = 2.
  2. Substitute point (4, 5): y − 5 = 2(x − 4)
  3. Expand: y − 5 = 2x − 8
  4. Add 5: y = 2x − 3
  5. Check: 2(4) − 3 = 5 ✓ and the gradient is 2, same as the original ✓

y = 2x − 3

Exam trapParallel lines have the same gradient but a different y-intercept. If your answer comes out identical to the original line, you have found the same line, not a parallel one, because the point you used must lie on the original.

8. Perpendicular lines

Perpendicular lines meet at a right angle (90°). Their gradients are linked by one rule: multiply them together and you always get −1.

lines are perpendicular if m₁ × m₂ = −1, so m₂ = −1 / m₁

Read it as: flip the gradient and change the sign.

Gradient of first lineGradient of the perpendicular line
2−1/2
3/4−4/3
−51/5
1−1

Check each row: 2 × (−1/2) = −1, 3/4 × (−4/3) = −1, and so on. A useful special case is the perpendicular bisector: the line that cuts a segment in half at 90°. Find the midpoint of the segment, find the negative reciprocal of its gradient, then use point–gradient form.

Worked example 11

Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining A(1, 3) and B(5, 5). Give your answer as y = mx + c.

  1. Midpoint of AB: ((1 + 5)/2, (3 + 5)/2) = (3, 4)
  2. Gradient of AB: (5 − 3)/(5 − 1) = 2/4 = 1/2
  3. Perpendicular gradient: flip and change sign: m = −2 (check: 1/2 × −2 = −1 ✓)
  4. Through (3, 4): y − 4 = −2(x − 3)
  5. Expand: y − 4 = −2x + 6, so y = −2x + 10

y = −2x + 10 (perpendicular to AB, through its midpoint (3, 4))

Exam tipTwo quick sanity checks on any perpendicular answer: the gradients must multiply to −1, and the line must pass through your point. Both take five seconds and catch most slips.
Exam trapDo not just stick a minus sign on the original gradient. The perpendicular to y = 2x + 1 is y = −½x + c, not y = −2x + c. Flip and change the sign. And remember both lines meet at exactly 90°.

9. Finding where two lines meet

The point where two lines cross is the single point that satisfies both equations. Since both expressions equal y, set them equal to each other, exactly like a pair of simultaneous equations.

set the two expressions for y equal, solve for x, then substitute back for y
Worked example 12

Find the coordinates where y = 2x + 1 and y = −x + 7 intersect.

  1. Set them equal: 2x + 1 = −x + 7
  2. Add x to both sides: 3x + 1 = 7
  3. Subtract 1: 3x = 6, so x = 2
  4. Substitute back into either equation: y = 2(2) + 1 = 5
  5. Check in the other equation: y = −(2) + 7 = 5 ✓

The lines meet at (2, 5)

Exam tipIf the equations are not both in the form y = ..., rearrange one of them first, or use elimination in the usual simultaneous-equations way. The intersection is just simultaneous equations with a coordinate meaning.
Exam trapA partial answer of x = 2 is not the point of intersection. Give the full coordinate pair (2, 5), and always substitute into the second equation to confirm both y-values match.

10. Practice set and answers

Do every question on paper first. Open an answer only after you have committed to one.

Set A · Coordinates, distance and midpoint

Write the equation of the horizontal line through (2, 5) and the vertical line through (−1, 3). State the gradient of each.
Horizontal line: y = 5, gradient m = 0. Vertical line: x = −1, it has no gradient (undefined).
Find the distance between A(−2, 5) and B(4, −1).
Horizontal gap: 4 − (−2) = 6. Vertical gap: −1 − 5 = −6. d = √(6² + (−6)²) = √(36 + 36) = √72 = 6√2 ≈ 8.49 units.
Find the midpoint of the segment joining (−3, 7) and (5, −1).
x: (−3 + 5) ÷ 2 = 1. y: (7 + (−1)) ÷ 2 = 3. Midpoint = (1, 3).
P(−2, −3) and Q(4, 5) are joined. Find the length of PQ and the coordinates of its midpoint.
Length: gaps are 6 and 8, so PQ = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10 units. Midpoint: ((−2 + 4)/2, (−3 + 5)/2) = (1, 1).

Set B · Gradient and y = mx + c

Find the gradient of the line through (−1, 4) and (3, −6).
m = (−6 − 4) ÷ (3 − (−1)) = −10 ÷ 4 = m = −2.5 (or −5/2). The line goes downhill.
State the gradient and y-intercept of y = 4x − 1, and give the coordinates where it crosses the y-axis.
m = 4, c = −1, crossing the y-axis at (0, −1).
Write the equation of the line with gradient −3 and y-intercept 7.
Substitute straight into y = mx + c: y = −3x + 7.
A line has equation y = 3 − 4x. Give its gradient and y-intercept.
Rearrange first: y = −4x + 3. So m = −4 and c = 3, crossing at (0, 3).

Set C · Equations of lines

Find the equation of the line with gradient 3 through (2, 7). Give your answer as y = mx + c.
y − 7 = 3(x − 2) → y − 7 = 3x − 6 → y = 3x + 1. Check: 3(2) + 1 = 7 ✓
Find the equation of the line through (−1, 4) and (3, 16).
Gradient: m = (16 − 4) ÷ (3 − (−1)) = 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Then y − 4 = 3(x + 1) → y − 4 = 3x + 3 → y = 3x + 7. Check: 3(3) + 7 = 16 ✓
Find the equation of the line parallel to y = −2x + 5 through (3, 1).
Parallel means the same gradient, so m = −2. y − 1 = −2(x − 3) → y − 1 = −2x + 6 → y = −2x + 7. Check: −2(3) + 7 = 1 ✓
Find the equation of the line perpendicular to y = ½x + 3 through (4, 4).
Perpendicular gradient: flip and change sign, m = −2 (check: ½ × −2 = −1 ✓). y − 4 = −2(x − 4) → y − 4 = −2x + 8 → y = −2x + 12. Check: −2(4) + 12 = 4 ✓
Find where the lines y = 2x − 3 and y = x + 1 intersect.
2x − 3 = x + 1 → x = 4. Then y = 4 + 1 = 5. Check in the first line: 2(4) − 3 = 5 ✓. Intersection at (4, 5).

11. Exam checklist

  • Read every point across then up: (x, y), and said which quadrant a point is in.
  • Used the distance formula as Pythagoras on the x-gap and y-gap, and gave a final number with units.
  • Found the midpoint by averaging each coordinate, and gave it as a bracketed point.
  • Stated the gradient as a value with a sign: m = −2, not just "negative".
  • Put y on top in the gradient formula: (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁).
  • Rewrote equations as y = mx + c before reading off m and c.
  • Gave every final equation in the form y = mx + c.
  • Used the same gradient for parallel lines, and m₂ = −1/m₁ for perpendicular lines.
  • Checked a perpendicular pair multiplies to −1, and that both lines meet at 90°.
  • Found intersections by solving simultaneous equations, then checked the point in both equations and wrote the answer as (x, y).
